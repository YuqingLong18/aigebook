7.7 The Challenges Facing Deep Learning: Explainability
1. Learning Objectives
(1) Understand the importance of neural network explainability and the challenges that come from its absence.
(2) Learn about local explanation and global explanation methods and how to apply them in practice.
(3) Explore the deeper reasons for the lack of explainability and the strategies to deal with it.

Another challenge that deep learning faces is the problem of lacking explainability, also known as the "black box mystery." We can only see the model's output, but we can't understand its internal processes.
The lack of explainability not only makes it harder for researchers to deeply understand how neural networks work inside, but it also limits their use in fields that require high reliability, such as healthcare, finance, and autonomous driving. For example, in an autonomous driving system, a decision-making process that we can't explain could cause serious car accidents, which in turn weakens people's trust in the technology. Therefore, opening up the neural network "black box" to explore its internal decision-making process and mechanisms has become a key problem that the field of artificial intelligence urgently needs to solve.

1.	What is Explainability of Neural Networks?
When we use a neural network to handle a task, we can only see the final result it gives us; the process in the middle is not transparent. Explainability refers to a system or model’s ability to provide clear, intuitive, and easy-to-understand reasons or evidence for its decisions. In the context of neural networks, explainability specifically asks if we can get insight into the process of how a neural network derives an output from the input data. To put it simply, it requires that we not only see "what the neural network did" but also understand "why it did it that way."
However, because neural networks have complex structures and non-linear characteristics, explaining their behavior is often very difficult. A neural network is made of a large number of interconnected neurons, and all these neurons work together to process the input data. As the layers get deeper, these transformations build up, creating non-linear decision paths and logic that are hard to trace. In other words, even if we knew every calculation step inside the network, it would still be hard to understand how these calculations combine to form a reasonable decision, and we wouldn't be able to explain why mistakes happen. This is the fundamental reason for the lack of explainability.
A lack of explainability is a significant weakness of neural networks. It not only gets in the way of our understanding of the model's internal logic but also directly affects how trustworthy and reliable the model is in real-world applications. For instance, in high-risk areas like medicine, finance, and autonomous driving, a decision that can't be explained is hard to accept, because people can't afford the consequences of a mistake, nor can they accept a situation where they can't trace the source of an error. Because of this, exploring the explainability of neural networks is not just an important direction for academic research; it's also a problem we must solve to promote the widespread use of neural networks in various fields.
Let’s take autonomous driving as an example. Currently, most self-driving systems rely on end-to-end deep learning models. These models directly map complex sensor data (like images from high-definition cameras, laser radar scans, etc.) to vehicle control commands, such as steering, accelerating, and braking. Although this end-to-end processing method has shown high efficiency in certain scenarios, its internal decision logic is not transparent, which creates safety risks that are difficult to understand and even harder to accept. For example, when a vehicle suddenly brakes without warning, passengers and other people on the road need to know exactly why this decision was made. Was it because it detected an obstacle ahead, a pedestrian suddenly appeared, or there was an internal vehicle malfunction? However, neural networks lack this ability to explain. They can’t give a reason, which means they can’t solve the problem, and the same issue might repeat itself. This would greatly damage people's trust in autonomous driving technology.

2.	Local Explanation Methods
Local explanation methods can provide some evidence for a model's decision on a specific sample. For example, why was a picture of an apple identified as an apple and not a cat? Why was a person walking mistakenly identified as a bird flying? This kind of "after-the-fact" explanation helps us analyze the basis for the network's decisions and find potential problems.
Visualization technology is a common local explanation method. It provides a basis for the model's decision by highlighting the areas that had a significant impact on the outcome. For example, in image processing, we can identify the activation values of different locations in a picture within the neural network. These activation values form a heatmap, where the intensity of the colors reveals how much attention the model paid to different areas when making its decision. For instance, in Figure 7-37, the heatmap highlights the regions in each picture that significantly influenced the decision. When the decision result is different, the highlighted significant region also changes. As in the first picture, when the content is identified as a "bookstore," the heatmap highlights the main entrance of the bookstore in the image. When the content is identified as a "car," the heatmap highlights the car in the image. Through the heatmap, we can analyze why the model made a certain decision, and at the same time, we can also analyze the reasons why the model made a wrong decision.

3.	Global Explanation Methods
A local explanation is an "individual" explanation for each sample, like one picture, a piece of audio, or a sentence. A global explanation means understanding the overall behavior of the model, such as the function of each layer of a neural network and its role after training is complete. As shown in Figure 7-38, by observing the patterns represented by the neurons in each layer of a convolutional neural network, we can see that from the shallow layers to the deep layers, the neurons' receptive fields gradually get bigger, and the patterns they represent become more and more complex and meaningful. For example, the first layer only shows some basic lines, the second layer produces some simple patterns, the fourth layer shows some objects or parts of objects, and by the fifth layer, some scenes appear. This change in patterns from low to high, from simple to complex, reflects the model's process of understanding objects and scenes, thus giving us a better understanding of the model's functions and decision-making process. Furthermore, by comparing two neural networks trained separately on pictures of scenery and pictures of objects, we can find that the patterns learned in the shallow layers are quite similar, while the higher layers show significant differences. This is consistent with human intuition.
Besides the visualization methods mentioned above, researchers have also proposed many other solutions. For example, there is a "proxy model" method, which involves training a simple, explainable model to imitate the behavior of a complex model. For instance, a method called LIME uses a linear model to approximate the decision-making behavior of a complex neural network and then determines the importance of various features by analyzing their weights in the linear model.

4.	Summary
One of the shortcomings of deep learning is its lack of explainability, which is difficult to accept for many applications. In recent years, people have paid more attention to this issue and have proposed some solutions. The methods discussed in this section can all be categorized as "post-hoc explanation," which means that after the model has been trained, we then try to explain its behavior on specific test samples or its overall decision-making behavior. Another approach is called "ante-hoc explainability," which aims to design a class of models that are inherently easy to explain. These models are still based on neural networks. The difference is that their network nodes are given specific meanings, which gives the model a certain degree of explainability. The performance of these models is generally weaker than mainstream models, but their greater explainability means they are more reassuring to use.
Although some progress has been made in the research of model explainability, these explanations cannot clearly present the reasons for a decision, nor can they provide reasonable solutions to fix problems. This is related to the intricate information processing happening inside the neural network and also to the extremely high degree of freedom the network has. While a very free neural network certainly provides powerful learning capabilities, it also allows the network to learn decision-making criteria and processes that are different from those of humans. These criteria and processes are inherently difficult to present to humans, which may be the fundamental reason for the lack of explainability.
Finally, some researchers have raised questions about explainability itself. They argue that the human nervous system also lacks explainability, but this has not affected people's trust in human decisions. In fact, even now, we know very little about the functions and behavioral processes of the human brain, let alone how to explain a specific process. Based on this, some researchers believe that "controllability" is a more realistic short-term goal than "explainability." For example, using multi-model fusion can improve the reliability of decisions. In this way, although the individual models themselves remain unexplainable, the overall risk is controllable. As artificial intelligence becomes more and more powerful, the requirements for controllability will also become increasingly strict in the future.
