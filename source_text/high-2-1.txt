Imagine someone who had read every book and document written throughout history—wouldn’t that person be considered extremely intelligent? Similarly, if a machine’s “brain” is filled with all kinds of knowledge, it could also become intelligent. This approach is called knowledge-based AI. This section will introduce how knowledge-based AI works and the achievements it has made.

1.	Knowledge and Intelligence
American psychologist Raymond Cattell proposed that human cognitive abilities can be divided into two types: fluid intelligence and crystallized intelligence.

Fluid intelligence refers to basic thinking abilities used in cognitive processes, such as comprehension, learning ability, and the capacity to solve new problems.

Crystallized intelligence is associated with accumulated knowledge, primarily reflected in the ability to use learned experiences and information to solve problems.

Fluid intelligence begins to decline after adulthood, while crystallized intelligence increases with age and accumulated knowledge (as shown in Figure 2-1).

The distinction between fluid and crystallized intelligence suggests that knowledge plays a crucial role in human intelligence. Human civilization has far outpaced the capabilities of other species, not just because of our reasoning abilities, but more importantly because of our collective accumulation of knowledge.
Consider if you traveled back in time just one century. Even without changing your profession, you'd appear extremely gifted, even like a genius. You might cure patients using modern medicine, invent captivating alcoholic drinks, or improve food production with advanced agricultural techniques. No matter the field, you’d become a “superstar” of that era thanks to what you learn as a matter of course. That’s the power of accumulated knowledge.

So, how can we impart that knowledge to machines, and what knowledge should we focus on teaching them? Historically, two main approaches have been tried: one is called general-knowledge-based AI, and the other is experience-based AI.

2.	General-Knowledge-Based AI
Nearly all rigorous academic systems (like mathematics and physics) are built upon a few fundamental axioms or laws. For example, Euclidean geometry is based on five axioms, and Newtonian mechanics is founded on three laws. Using these as general knowledge, vast bodies of new knowledge can be derived through limited reasoning rules. This was the approach taken in early AI: provide machines with general knowledge and let them reason through problems. This method is the aforementioned general-knowledge-based AI. Its well-known achievements include automated theorem proving and man-machine game playing.

1)Theorem Proving
Allen Newell and Herbert Simon’s “Logic Theorist” was the first general-purpose automated theorem-proving system. Based on five axioms, it proved 38 out of the first 52 theorems in Chapter 2 of Principia Mathematica by Russell and Whitehead. This topic was discussed in Section 1.6 and will not be repeated here.

The basic idea of theorem proving is to derive new theorems step by step from basic axioms using reasoning rules until the target theorem is reached.

Figure 2-2 shows an example of deriving a new theorem from known ones. This example uses syllogism (also known as premise-based reasoning). For example, if “X is even → X is divisible by 2” is true, and “4 is even” is true, then we can conclude “4 is divisible by 2.”

Theorem proving is essentially a search process: it begins with established theorems and facts and aims to logically build to the theorem to be proved. This process is often time-consuming, much like solving complex math problems: many approaches need to be considered, and each path represents its own search direction. To speed up this process, researchers designed a more efficient algorithm called heuristic search. This algorithm estimates which reasoning paths are most likely to lead to the target theorem and prioritizes those paths.

Figure 2-3 illustrates a heuristic search example. Starting from point A, the system tries to find a path to the goal G. At each step, it decides which node to expand next. However, the exact distance from nodes like B and C to G is unknown. Heuristic search uses prior knowledge to estimate these distances, allowing the algorithm to more effectively choose which node to explore next. Heuristic search was a key method in early AI.

How is heuristic search used in theorem proving? First, treat reasoning states as nodes. Choosing a theorem or fact to reason with becomes the node expansion process. Then, define heuristic information to evaluate the similarity between the current reasoning state and the target theorem. With these in place, heuristic search can be applied to prove theorems.

Note that what we’ve discussed is a basic version called forward search. In practice, researchers have developed many more strategies, like backward search starting from the goal, and resolution-based methods founded on proof by contradiction.

2)Man-Machine Game Playing
Man-machine game playing is another major milestone in early AI. Researchers including Turing, Shannon, and McCarthy all made significant contributions in this area.

A key algorithm used in these games is the minimax algorithm (see Figure 2-4). Its principle is to assume the opponent always makes the best move for themselves (which, in a two-player game, is worst for us), and then determine our best counter-move accordingly. In simple terms, it’s about finding the move most beneficial to us under the assumption that the opponent plays optimally.

Building on this, McCarthy and others proposed the alpha-beta pruning algorithm (see Figure 2-5), which is an optimization of the minimax algorithm that improves efficiency by cutting off unnecessary search paths.

3)Limitations of General Knowledge
General knowledge expressed through axioms and theorems is highly universal, embodying the distilled product of humanity’s collective exploration of nature. However, this general knowledge is often too basic, and deriving real-world phenomena from it requires immense computation. For example, physicists understand atomic properties and bonding rules. In theory, we can calculate the properties of any molecule, but in reality, even slightly complex molecules exceed the limits of our current computational power. Take proteins, for example. They are made of many atoms and have extremely complex structures. Though their properties can theoretically be calculated from fundamental physics, the computational cost is so high that it’s essentially impossible (see Figure 2-6).

Additionally, the real world is very complex, and many phenomena can’t be directly deduced from basic laws. First, beyond fundamental laws, there are also domain-specific rules. Second, new rules emerge at different observational scales. Third, there is much uncertainty in actual reality. For these three reasons, general knowledge alone cannot account for the complexities of the real world.

Discussion: Some have tried to predict weather using Newtonian mechanics alone, but this proved very difficult. Why might general-knowledge-based AI methods succeed in theorem proving but not in real-world tasks like weather forecasting?

Explanation: Theorem proving is a closed task (deriving geometric theorems from known axioms within a well-defined system), and the goal is close to the known knowledge. In contrast, real-world systems are open-ended, full of uncertainty, and far removed from basic axioms, making them much harder to solve with fundamental laws alone.

3.	Empirical-Knowledge-Based AI
Empirical knowledge is the practical knowledge, built upon foundational laws, gained from real-world practice. Examples include a doctor’s clinical experience, a worker’s machine repair skills, and an accountant’s auditing practices. This type of knowledge is hard to derive strictly from basic rules but often solves real-world problems more directly and is thus very valuable.

1)Representing Empirical Knowledge
Empirical knowledge is often expressed in “if...then...” statements, such as: “If it’s cloudy and the humidity is high, then it will rain.” This format is called a production rule.

Connecting multiple production rules forms a chain of reasoning. For example: “If it’s cloudy and humid, then it will rain”; “If it rains, the river will flood”; “If the river floods, it may submerge houses.” (See Figure 2-7.) Therefore, starting from “cloudy and humid,” one can infer “houses may be submerged.”

2)Expert Systems
Expert systems organize expert knowledge, often as production rules, so that computers can reason and make decisions like human experts.

Thousands of expert systems have been developed and are widely used in chemistry, mathematics, physics, biology, medicine, agriculture, meteorology, geology, military, engineering, law, business, and many other fields (see Figure 2-8).

Expert systems consist of two main components: a knowledge base and an inference engine (see Figure 2-9). The knowledge base stores domain-specific knowledge, while the inference engine performs reasoning and decision-making. A complete expert system also includes interpreters, input/output modules, and interfaces for user interaction.

3)Knowledge Graphs
Knowledge graphs are a simple and efficient way to represent knowledge. They use graph structures in which nodes represent entities like people or events, and edges represent relationships between them. This form of knowledge representation is called a knowledge graph.

Figure 2-10 shows an example, including facts like “Leonardo da Vinci is the painter of the Mona Lisa” and “The Mona Lisa is housed in the Louvre in France.” With such knowledge, one can answer questions like “Does James live in Paris?” or “When is his birthday?”
Google first proposed the concept of the knowledge graph on May 17, 2012, aiming to build a supplemental knowledge base to enhance search engine capabilities. Powered by this knowledge base, search engines can provide users more accurate information.

Section Summary
Knowledge-based AI teaches machines human knowledge, allowing them to solve problems through reasoning algorithms. Based on general knowledge, AI can handle tasks with clear rules and goals, such as board games or theorem proving, but struggles with complex real-world problems. Empirical-Knowledge-based AI can tackle more complex, specialized tasks and is widely used in medical diagnosis, drug discovery, and more. However, it has limitations: knowledge base design is difficult and costly to maintain.

Since the 21st century, the growth of the internet has led to a large-scale accumulation of data, making data-driven AI increasingly dominant. Still, knowledge-based AI remains important, especially in domains with scarce data, where human knowledge remains essential. In areas such as healthcare and law, where decision-making must be highly controlled, knowledge-based approaches are still valued for their interpretability and controllability.
