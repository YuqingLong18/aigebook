With the rise of the internet, more and more data has become available, and the connectionist school has had a wider spaceideal circumstances for its application, now becoming the mainstream approach in artificial intelligence. The artificial neural network is the primary modeling tool of the connectionist school. It simulates the human brain's nervous system, connecting simple computational units to achieve complex functionalities. 

 

As the core method of modern artificial intelligence technology, the importance of artificial neural networks is self-evident. This section will focus on the core ideas and developmental history of neural networks. Specific knowledge about network structures will be detailed in the next section. 

 

Neural Networks in the Brain 

The human brain consists of 50 to 100 billion neurons. Each neuron is homogeneous, with a simple structure and a single function. However, when a massive number of neurons are connected, they can perform extremely complex functions. The connections between neurons are called synapses, and the strength of these synaptic connections can be adjusted, which gives the neural network its ability to learn. 

 

At birth, the synaptic connections between neurons in an infant's brain are very dense, but not all connections are used frequently. During the learning process, frequently used synaptic connections are strengthened, while unused connections gradually weaken or disappear. This process is called "neural pruning." The purpose of neural pruning is to enable the brain to process information more efficiently. Therefore, although an adult brain has fewer synaptic connections than an infant's, its connections are more orderly and functional. As shown in Figure 2-43, after a baby is born, the number of neuron connections in the brain gradually increases with age. However, after the age of four, the number of connections starts to decrease, while the structure becomes continuously optimized. 

 

The Artificial Neural Network Model 

Inspired by biological neural networks, American computational neuroscientists Warren McCulloch and Walter Pitts proposed the artificial neural network (ANN) model in 1943, initiating research into artificial neural networks. They believed that if humans could acquire intelligence through neural networks, machines could achieve intelligence in a similar way. McCulloch and Pitts discovered that while this neuron model is simple, a network composed of many such neurons could perform any logical calculus, thus possessing the ability to simulate human thought. This finding laid the initial theoretical foundation for artificial neural networks. 

 

The M-P neuron model is a high-level simplification of a biological neuron, primarily intended to provide a mathematical model to verify the logical computation capabilities of neural networks. This model is still very simple and cannot describe the complexity and dynamic characteristics of biological neurons. In particular, the M-P neuron model lacks the ability to learn, making it incomplete. Nevertheless, the work of McCulloch and Pitts opened a new direction for achieving intelligence by simulating the human nervous system, marking a significant milestone in AI development. 

 

The Perceptron and Linearly Inseparable Problems 

Rosenblatt's Perceptron 

In 1958, Frank Rosenblatt implemented the first learnable artificial neural network—the Perceptron—on an IBM 704 computer. The Perceptron model is a single-layer neural network. Information from the input layer is weighted and summed at the output layer, then passed through an activation function ƒ( ) to produce a binary result (0/1). This model is very similar to the M-P model, with the key difference being Rosenblatt's introduction of learnable weights, which allows the model to adapt to specific tasks through learning. Rosenblatt designed a training algorithm that gradually adjusts the network's weights through an iterative process, making the model's output as close as possible to the data's actual labels (the desired output). Rosenblatt also implemented the Perceptron model on specialized hardware called the Mark 1 Perceptron, which was used to recognize letters and numbers in images. 

 

The Limitations of the Perceptron 

The emergence of the Perceptron attracted widespread attention, especially for its learning capabilities. Some scholars, including Rosenblatt, believed that, given time, such a learnable neural network could acquire a vast amount of knowledge and thus achieve powerful intelligence. 

 

This expectation proved to be overly optimistic. In 1969, another pioneer of artificial intelligence and one of the organizers of the Dartmouth Workshop, Marvin Minsky, along with his colleague Seymour Papert, published the book Perceptrons, which pointed out the limitations of the Perceptron model. This critique led to a downturn in neural network research. Minsky's criticisms of the Perceptron were multifaceted, but the most crucial one was proving that the Perceptron could not solve linearly inseparable problems. A linearly inseparable problem can be simply understood as a problem that cannot be classified by a single straight line or a plane. The exclusive OR (XOR) operation in mathematical logic is a classic example of a linearly inseparable problem. 

 

As shown in Figure 2-45, the red squares and blue circles represent the results of an XOR operation on logical variables [Equation] and [Equation](red square for 1, blue circle for 0). It is clear that no single straight line can completely separate the squares from the circles, meaning the XOR operation is a linearly inseparable problem. Minsky proved that any such linearly inseparable problem, even one as simple as XOR, cannot be solved by a Perceptron. Minsky's critique exposed the shortcomings of the Perceptron model, prompting researchers to delve deeper into the learning capabilities of neural networks and providing important theoretical considerations for subsequent developments.

The Backpropagation Algorithm 

It is not difficult to understandhard to see that increasing the number of layers in a Perceptron model enhances its expressive power and learning ability, but training a multi-layer network is not easy. In 1986, David Rumelhart, Geoffrey Hinton, and Ronald Williams published a paper in Nature that applied the backpropagation algorithm (BP), originally proposed by Paul Werbos in 1974, to train neural networks, making the learning of multi-layer perceptrons possible. It can be said that without the backpropagation algorithm, it would be impossible to effectively train multi-layer neural networks, and we would not have the powerful artificial intelligence of today. 

 

The backpropagation algorithm is essentially a gradient descent algorithm. Its special feature is that it leverages the hierarchical structure of neural networks, adjusting connection weights starting from the last layer and then moving backward layer by layer until it reaches the first layer. This parameter adjustment process proceeds from back to front, hence the name backpropagation algorithm. 

 

Figure 2-47 shows an example of the backpropagation algorithm. Input data [Equation] passes through the hidden layer[Equation]and produces an output [Equation] at the output layer. This output is compared with the target value to calculate a loss function, such as[Equation]. The backpropagation algorithm calculates the gradient of the loss function with respect to each parameter and uses the gradient descent algorithm to adjust the connection weights of the neural network. Unlike the standard gradient descent algorithm, a neural network has a layered structure, so when calculating the gradient for a certain layer, it can reuse the gradients already computed for the subsequent layer, thus avoiding redundant calculations and improving efficiency. Specifically, the gradients for the weights connecting the hidden layer to the output layer are calculated first (red dashed lines), and these gradients are then reused to calculate the gradients for the weights connecting the input layer to the hidden layer (blue dashed lines). This process starts from the weights of the last layer and proceeds backward, step by step, to the weights of the first layer. This is the principle of the backpropagation algorithm.

Section Summary 

The human central nervous system, especially the neural system in the brain, is the material basis of human intelligence. The simple idea behind artificial neural network research is that by simulating the brain's neural structure, it might be possible to achieve a similarly powerful intelligence. This research has evolved from the initial M-P model to today's deep learning technology, undergoing several transformations. In summary, Rosenblatt introduced the learnable Perceptron model, the introduction of the backpropagation algorithm made the training of complex neural networks possible, and the emergence of deep neural networks allowed the powerful learning capabilities of neural networks to be fully realized. Today, deep neural networks have become the core tool in the field of artificial intelligence, achieving remarkable success in areas like speech recognition, image processing, and text analysis, and making continuous breakthroughs in interdisciplinary fields such as physics, chemistry, and mathematics. Not only that, deep neural networks also form the backbone of various large models today and are the foundation for building human-like or even superhuman intelligent agents in the future.
