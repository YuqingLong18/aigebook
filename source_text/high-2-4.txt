2.4 Machine Learning Methods

Learning Objectives
(1)	Understand the basic principles of supervised learning, unsupervised learning, and reinforcement learning, and clarify their respective application scenarios and characteristics.
(2)	Grasp the basic idea of supervised learning, understand the difference between classification and regression tasks, and recognize the importance of data labeling in supervised learning.
(3)	Understand the basic approaches in unsupervised learning, master the core ideas and value of clustering and manifold learning.
(4)	Understand the principles and characteristics of reinforcement learning, grasp how reinforcement learning is applied in multi-step decision-making tasks, and clarify the role of environmental feedback in strategy optimization.
(5)	Analyze the differences and connections between the three learning methods, and reflect on how to choose the appropriate learning method for different real-world scenarios.

Humans learn in a variety of ways: by listening to teachers, by observing and researching independently, or by learning through trial and error. Similar approaches also exist in machine learning. In this section, we will introduce three representative machine learning methods: supervised learning, unsupervised learning, and reinforcement learning. These methods correspond to different learning situations and are suitable for different goal-oriented tasks, as shown in Figure 2-26. Understanding these methods provides a comprehensive overview of machine learning.
1.	Supervised Learning
Supervised learning is like a teacher giving a lecture in class, imparting knowledge to students. In machine learning, humans act as the teacher, and machines as the students. How is this knowledge passed on? By labeling data, —for instance, labeling an image of an apple as “apple,” and one of an orange as “orange.” Through being givenWith such labeled data, the machine receives human guidance and learns what an apple looks like and what an orange looks like.
Overall, supervised learning includes two types of tasks: classification and regression.
(1) Classification Tasks
As the name suggests, classification tasks involve determining the category a sample belongs to (Figure 2-27). For example: identifying whether an animal in a picture is a cat or a dog; determining whether a spoken sound is "ah" or "oh"; recognizing whether a handwritten digit is a 1 or a 9; or classifying an emoji as angry or happy.
A machine learning system used for classification outputs a label predicting the sample’s category. Sometimes it also outputs a confidence score for that prediction. The learning goal of a classification task is to improve the accuracy of category predictions.
(2) Regression Tasks
Unlike classification, regression tasks predict continuous numerical values rather than discrete labels. You can think of it as predicting a certain property of a sample based on its features. For example, if a machine is given a dataset of house sizes and prices, it can learn the relationship between them and predict the price of a house based on its size (Figure 2-28).
Another example: if the machine is given weather data, such as satellite images, temperature, humidity, air pressure, geographical features, and historical records, it can predict the temperature in the coming days.
(3) The Importance of Data Labeling
In supervised learning, data labeling is crucial. It’s like doing practice questions in a textbook—having the correct answer key helps us learn better. Likewise, only accurately labeled data enables the machine to learn the correct knowledge correctly.
The difficulty of data labeling varies by task. For example, in image recognition, labeling might involve drawing boxes around objects and tagging their categories, which is relatively easy. But in speech recognition, it may involve transcribing spoken sentences, which is much harder. Although labeling is time-consuming and labor-intensive, it is the indispensable “fuel” of machine learning—indispensable.
2.	Unsupervised Learning
Unsupervised learning is like a student learning on their own self-studying without a teacher. In this method, the machine isn’t provideddoesn’t have labeled “correct answers” to refer to, but—it must rather discover patterns within the data on its own. Unsupervised learning primarily includes two types of tasks: clustering and manifold learning.
(1) Clustering
Clustering involves grouping similar samples together, so that items in the same group are alike in certain features. For example, in Figure 2-29, apples, peaches, and bananas are clustered into three groups. We don’t necessarily know the names of the fruits in each group; we simply group similar ones together based on appearance.
K-means is a widely used clustering algorithm (Figure 2-30). It first defines K cluster centers, and then assigns each sample to the nearest center. Then, it recalculates the center of each cluster and reassigns the samples based on these updated centers. This process repeats until the samples are grouped into K distinct subsets, where samples within each subset are as similar as possible, and samples between different subsets are as different as possible.
You can imagine this process like this: from a pile of fruits, randomly pick a few to act as "group leaders." Then assign the remaining fruits to the nearest group based on features like color, shape, and size. Next, calculate the average color, shape, and size of each group, and treat those averages as new "group leaders." Repeat this process until all fruits are grouped properly.
Clustering algorithms have many applications. For example, in product recommendation, clustering similar products allows the system to suggest items similar to a user’s purchase history.
(2) Manifold Learning
Manifold learning is another unsupervised learning method. It aims to uncover low-dimensional structures in high-dimensional data. As shown in Figure 2-31, the data may exist in three dimensions, but it is actually distributed on a two-dimensional curved surface (like a Sswiss roll). This curved surface embedded in a higher-dimensional space is called a manifold. If we can find this surface, we can better represent the relationships between data points, as—because distances on the manifold are more meaningful than distances in the original space.
You can imagine running on a track. The track’s shape represents the data’s manifold. If you want to calculate the distance to the person ahead of you, you should measure it along the track, not in a straight line through the air.
Manifold learning is often used for data compression. In the Sswiss roll example, although the data is 3D, its important features lie in 2D. So representing it in two dimensions is sufficient. This compression is called dimensionality reduction. It simplifies data and makes it easier for machines to learn.
Another benefit of dimensionality reduction is visualization. Most machine learning data is high-dimensional and hard to understand. Manifold learning reduces the data to 2D or 3D, making it easier for humans to observe the distribution. Figure 2-32 shows handwritten digits (0–9) mapped to 2D space using manifold learning, making the data’s structure visually clear.
3.	Reinforcement Learning
Reinforcement learning is a feedback-based learning method, similar to how humans learn by trial and error. In terms of learning signals, reinforcement learning lies between supervised and unsupervised learning. It doesn’t have direct signals like labeled categories, but learns by interacting with the environment, receiving rewards or punishments, and adjusting behavior to reach a learning goal.
(1) Basic Principle
An fun example of reinforcement learning is training a puppy to fetch the right fruit (Figure 2-33). You want the dog to fetch the correct fruit when you give a command, but the dog doesn’t understand human language directly. What can you do? Use rewards to guide it: give a treat (like a bone) when it gets it right; give nothing when it gets it wrong. After enough tries, the dog learns to fetch the right fruit based on your commands.
(2) Suitable Tasks
Reinforcement learning is particularly suited for multi-step decision-making tasks, such as robot locomotion, investment strategy optimization, and board games. These tasks require a sequence of decisions, withwhere the final outcome depends on the accumulation of those choices.
Imagine playing a game of Go. Every move you make gets a response from your opponent, —sometimes gaining advantage, sometimes losing ground. You adjust your overall strategy based on this feedback of advantage and disadvantage. A skilled player doesn’t just focus on individual moves, but aims for the overall win. Reinforcement learning works the same way: it continuously refines strategy through environmental feedback to maximize long-term rewards.
This long-term focus is why reinforcement learning excels at complex, time-dependent problems. It's also the reason why AlphaGo, using deep reinforcement learning, was able to defeat top human players.
Section Summary
This section introduced three main machine learning methods: supervised learning, unsupervised learning, and reinforcement learning.
Supervised learning is like classroom learning under a teacher’s guidance, suitable for well-defined tasks like classification and regression. Unsupervised learning resembles self-study, uncovering structures in data distributions, with clustering and manifold learning as typical tasks. Reinforcement learning mimics experiential learning through trial and error, well-suited for tasks requiring sequential decision-making.
Understanding the fundamental ideas behind each method helps form a systematic view of machine learning. Recognizing their typical applications also provides practical guidance when tackling real-world problems.
