2.8 Deep Learning Basics
Learning Objectives
(1) Understand the basic concepts of deep learning, clarify the characteristics and application value of deep neural networks, and grasp the differences between deep learning and traditional machine learning.
(2) Recognize the difficulties in training deep neural networks, and understand the role and limitations of the backpropagation algorithm.
(3) Master Hinton's proposed pre-training method, understand how it effectively solves the challenge of training deep neural networks, and recognize its historical significance.
(4) Understand the hierarchical feature learning process of deep learning, clarify the principle of abstractness and conceptualization of high-level features, and grasp the importance of hierarchical feature extraction.
(5) Recognize the factors behind the success of deep learning, analyzing the key roles of big data, computing resources, and open-source sharing in driving its development.
 
This section will introduce the basic concepts of deep learning. Deep learning is a machine learning method based on multi-layer neural networks. Researchers have found that as the number of network layers increases, not only does the model becomes more expressive, allowing it to better handle complex classification or fitting problems, but more importantly, it can simulate the human brain's hierarchical information processing. This shift transformed the neural network from a simple function-fitting tool into an information processing system akin to the human brain. This progress in thought not only returned to the original intentions of the connectionist school but also laid the model and algorithm foundation for a new generation of artificial intelligence driven by big data.
1. Deep Neural Networks
1) Basic Concepts
A neural network containing more than two hidden layers is called a deep neural network (DNN), as shown in Figure 2-61. Generally speaking, given the same total number of neurons, making a network deeper rather than wider results in stronger expressive power, which means deep neural networks theoretically possess a more powerful learning capability.
Machine learning methods based on multi-layer structures are often referred to as deep learning. In theory, any multi-layer structure can be used for deep learning, but deep neural networks are currently the most widely used deep learning model. Therefore, deep learning can be simply understood as the learning process based on deep neural networks. Figure 2-62 illustrates the relationship between the concepts of artificial intelligence, machine learning, neural networks, and deep learning.
2) Difficulties in Training Deep Neural Networks
Although researchers realized early on that multi-layer neural networks had stronger expressive power, research on deep neural networks was not prioritized for a long time. There are likely two main reasons for this. One is that the universal approximation theorem tells us that even a simple network with one hidden layer can learn any complex continuous function, as long as it has a sufficient number of hidden neurons. Therefore, pursuing deeper networks seemed to have little theoretical significance. The second point is more important: in practice, training multi-layer networks is very difficult, and the results are often worse than those of a three-layer network. This gradually led to a loss of interest in deep neural networks.
Why is training deep neural networks more difficult? This is mainly because neural network training relies on the backpropagation algorithm, which is essentially a gradient descent method. The principle of gradient descent is to iteratively search for a local minimum of the loss function based on the gradient. If the loss function is very complex, with many local minima on its surface, gradient descent can easily get stuck in one local minimum, unable to reach a more ideal optimal position. Therefore, although deep neural networks have theoretical advantages, their actual performance is not ideal.
Figure 2-63 demonstrates the surface of the loss function for a deep neural network, VGG-56. The two-dimensional coordinates of each point on the surface represent the network's parameter values, and the height represents the loss function's value at those parameters. The training goal of this network is to find the lowest point on this surface starting from a random position. As can be seen, the surface has a large number of small valleys and saddle-like plateau regions, making training extremely difficult. This is also why neural networks with more than three layers were rarely used before 2006.
Extended Reading: Universal Approximation Theorem
The neural network is a powerful computational model. Research has shown that even a simple neural network with a single hidden layer can approximate any continuous function, as long as it has enough nodes in its hidden layer, demonstrating a powerful learning ability. This conclusion is known as the universal approximation theorem.
A simple proof of the universal approximation theorem is provided in Figure 2-64. The left diagram shows that by using a step function as the activation function for the hidden nodes, a small network with two hidden nodes can represent a rectangular window function, where T_1 and T_2 are the activation thresholds of the two hidden nodes. The right diagram shows that by combining multiple pairs of hidden nodes, any continuous function can be approximated. Specifically, the input x, passing through step functions at two different positions, T_1 and T_2, can form a rectangular window function (left diagram). By combining multiple rectangular window functions (bottom right diagram, where each pair of neurons marked by a red circle is combined as shown in the left diagram to form a rectangular window function), any continuous function can be approximated (top right). The more hidden nodes there are, the higher the precision of this approximation.
3) Hinton's Pre-training Method
In 2006, Geoffrey Hinton from the University of Toronto proposed a pre-training method that solved the training problem for deep neural networks. Hinton's method involved first training a shallow network called a Restricted Boltzmann Machine (RBM) and then stacking these shallow networks layer by layer to form a pre-trained deep network. After pre-training, only a small amount of data was needed for fine-tuning to obtain a high-performance deep neural network.
The left part of the figure 2-65 shows the pre-training process: three independent two-layer models (Restricted Boltzmann Machines) are trained from bottom to top. Each layer's model takes the output of the previous layer as its input to extract higher-level information. The specific steps are as follows: ① Train RBM1, fix this model, and use it to process the input data to obtain the first layer of features, denoted as F1; ② With F1 as input, train RBM2, fix this model, and use RBM2 to process F1 to obtain the second layer of features, F2; ③ With F2 as input, train RBM3.
The right part of the figure shows the fine-tuning process: these three two-layer models are connected sequentially to form an encoder (the lower half), and then this connected model is inverted to serve as a decoder (the upper half), resulting in a pre-trained deep autoencoder. Based on this pre-trained network, all parameters are unfrozen for fine-tuning, with the goal of reconstructing the input image.
Hinton discovered that this pre-training and fine-tuning scheme could effectively train multi-layer neural networks, performing far better than shallow networks of a similar scale. This discovery was the starting point of deep learning and opened the door for a new generation of artificial intelligence technology based on deep neural networks.
Extended Reading: Restricted Boltzmann Machine
The Restricted Boltzmann Machine (RBM) is a generative stochastic neural network capable of learning the probability distribution of input data. It was proposed by Geoffrey Hinton and others and is widely used for unsupervised learning tasks such as dimensionality reduction and feature extraction.
In general, an RBM consists of an input layer and a hidden layer. The nodes between the two layers can be interconnected, but there are no connections between nodes within the same layer (hence, it is "restricted"). The weights of these connections determine the probability distribution of the input nodes. When we learn from training data, the learning objective is to adjust the RBM's parameters so that the probability distribution of its input nodes matches the distribution of the training data. Once training is complete, this RBM represents the distribution of the observed data. At this point, for any given input data x, a feature vector h^* can be obtained from the hidden layer, and this feature vector h represents the "features" of that input data.
4) The Flourishing Development of Deep Learning
Since Hinton proposed the pre-training method, numerous scholars have entered this research field, making deep learning the most active direction in artificial intelligence. In 2012, the deep neural network AlexNet, developed by Hinton's team, achieved a resounding victory in the ImageNet image classification competition, proving the power of deep neural networks. AlexNet is an 8-layer convolutional neural network. Through AlexNet, Hinton's team demonstrated that the depth of the model is crucial for performance improvement (Figure 2-66).
Yoshua Bengio, Geoffrey Hinton, and Yann LeCun are the leading figures in deep learning. Under their leadership, deep learning has made remarkable achievements in multiple fields. In 2018, the three of them were jointly awarded the Turing Award, the highest honor in computer science, for their outstanding contributions to the field of deep learning.
2. Hierarchical Feature Learning
1) What Are High-Level Features?
The power of a deep neural network lies in its ability to progressively extract high-level features, or abstract features, through hierarchical processes. Taking an image recognition convolutional neural network as an example, as shown in Figure 2-67, the lower layers of the network learn simple lines, while the higher layers learn more global patterns. This indicates that the network can extract features layer by layer, and the higher the layer, the more global and semantically clear the features become. Such features are called high-level features.
High-level features are also sometimes called abstract features. "Abstract" refers to having a clear, conceptualized meaning, such as "Zhang San's face" or "a Labrador's head." These conceptualized semantics are not specific representations but are abstractions of these specific forms. For example, Zhang San's face could be a side profile or a front view, a face with a hat, or a face with glasses. Regardless of the form of expression, they all belong to the concept of "Zhang San's face." Through hierarchical processing, deep learning extracts and summarizes these different representations, finally representing the abstract concept in the upper layers of the network to form a high-level feature.
2) An Example with a Convolutional Neural Network for Face Recognition
Let's observe a convolutional neural network used for face recognition. We can see that in the lower layers of the network, neurons are activated by simple lines. However, in the higher layers, what activates the neurons are larger-scale facial patterns. This shows that the network extracts features of different scales at different levels, and the features obtained further along are more advanced and have a stronger relevance to the task of face recognition, as shown in figure 2-68.

Think About It:
(1) In a deep convolutional neural network, why do the features become more high-levelabstracted in the deeper layers?
(2) Why do the features in the later layers become more strongly related to the specific task the network is being trained for?
Answer:
	As you go deeper into the network, the "receptive field" of the convolutional layers gets larger. This allows it to learn feature patterns that are more complex and have a clearer meaning.
	The entire goal of training the network is to solve a specific problem, whether it's identifying cats in photos or translating languages. To achieve this goal, the network's training process automatically adjusts itself to pay more attention to the features that are most helpful for that particular task and ignore the ones that aren't.

3) The "Shared Bottom Layers, Varied Top Layers" Pattern
Figure 2-69 shows four hierarchical features deep neural networks learned. These four networks are used to recognize faces, cars, elephants, and chairs, respectively. It can be observed that the features extracted by these four networks in the bottom layers are very similar, while the features in the top layers begin to differ. The reason for this phenomenon is that images of all objects are composed of simple lines, and only at a larger scale do these lines combine to form the contours of different objects. This is reflected in convolutional neural network learning as a pattern of shared features in the lower layers and distinct features in the higher layers.
4) Comparison with Human Information Processing
Interestingly, researchers have found that the hierarchical processing of deep neural networks bears a strong resemblance to the information processing in the human brain. For example, studies have found that the feature extraction process of a convolutional neural network used for image recognition is very similar to the visual processing in the human brain. As shown in the figure 2-70, the activation of different areas in the human brain seems to correspond to the activation of specific layers in a neural network. For instance, the patterns learned by the neural network (middle) correspond to different regions of the human brain (right). Early visual areas (like V1) are better described by lower-level convolutional layers, while later visual areas (like V4) are better described by higher-level convolutional layers. This research provides evidence for the validity of deep learning from another perspective.

Section Summary
Deep neural networks have achieved tremendous success in many fields, including computer vision, computational auditory, natural language processing, and robotics. This success can be largely attributed to their powerful hierarchical feature learning capabilities. Through hierarchical feature learning, neural networks combine simple features into higher-level features of a larger scale. This composition not only significantly enhances the expressive power of the network but, more importantly, enables the learning of abstract features with task-invariant properties. Abstraction is a prerequisite for the formation of "concepts" and a critical step toward the emergence of intelligence. By means of hierarchical processing, deep learning acquires this abstraction, thereby possessing powerful comprehension and generation abilities.
Today, we understand that the pre-training scheme initially proposed by Hinton is not essential. Provided that the volume of data is sufficiently large and computational resources are abundant, powerful deep neural network models can be successfully trained. From this perspective, the success of deep learning relies not only on the inherent power of the algorithms themselves but also benefits from the accumulation of big data and the significant improvement in computational power, which have made large-scale machine learning possible.
Finally, open source has become a general consensus in the industry. Researchers upload their code, data, papers, models, and other resources to public websites, making them available for scientists worldwide to study and use free of charge. This has greatly promoted technical exchange and progress, leaving a unique historical mark on the current wave of artificial intelligence.
